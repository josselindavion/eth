Introduction to the topic and first understanding of the SHPB :

**The parts.** From left to right: the striker (bar launched at speed V), the input bar (only transmits the pulse, its single gauge just triggers the camera), the LID pusher (L-shaped part attached to the right end of the specimen), the specimen, and the output bar placed above the input bar, pointing left, clamped on the left end of the specimen, carrying the gauge that measures the force. A high-speed camera films the specimen. Striker, input bar and pusher have the same impedance.

**The rules to remember.** When a wave reaches the end of a bar, everything depends on what is behind it. Same impedance: the wave passes through entirely, no reflection. Nothing behind (or something very weak): free end, the wave comes back with its sign flipped and the end moves twice as fast as the material in the wave. A wall behind: fixed end, the wave comes back with the same sign and the end doesn't move. And a simple contact transmits compression but not tension: if tension arrives, the parts separate.

**Before impact.** The striker flies at V as one block, with no stress. There is no wave anywhere yet.

**The impact.** The front of the striker is slowed down, the end of the bar is set in motion, and both end up at V/2, pressed against each other. Two compressive waves are born at the interface and leave back to back: the incident wave going right into the bar, and a wave going left into the striker (the rear of the striker doesn't know yet that the front has hit, so it keeps going and piles up on it, like in a car pile-up).

**The round trip in the striker sets the pulse duration.** The wave travelling back through the striker reaches its rear end, which is free, and comes back to the right as an unloading wave. Behind the unloading wave, the striker is at rest and stress-free. As long as it hasn't reached the front, the front is still compressed and keeps pushing the bar. When it reaches the interface, the whole striker is at rest and it separates on its own, without bouncing (like the ball in Newton's cradle). The unloading wave passes into the bar and becomes the end of the pulse. So the pulse is a square pulse lasting 2L_striker/c: long striker for ~100 s⁻¹, short striker for ~1000 s⁻¹.

**The incident wave up to the pusher.** Same impedance everywhere, so it travels through the input bar and the pusher without reflecting, up to the end where the specimen is attached.

**The big reflection at the pusher.** Behind the pusher, only the specimen resists, and it's a tiny sheet compared to a 20 mm bar, holding only a few kN. It's almost a free end: 80% or more of the wave turns back as tension into the input bar (the reflected wave), and the pusher moves at about 2 × V/2 = V, roughly constant during the whole pulse. Moving to the right, it pulls on the specimen, whose other end is held by the output bar: the specimen is loaded in tension at quasi-constant speed. That's the compression → tension inversion. Small flaw at the start: the massive end of the pusher briefly acts as an obstacle (the very beginning of the reflection is compressive) and the pusher bends a bit, which gives a small velocity peak, then it settles.

**The transmitted wave.** The specimen, being pulled, pulls in turn on the output bar: a small tensile wave travels left into the output bar.

**The measurements.** The gauge on the output bar, close to the specimen, sees the transmitted wave pass; the bar stays elastic so F = A·E·ε, with a dispersion correction to bring the signal back to the interface with the specimen. The camera films the speckle pattern and digital image correlation gives the displacement between two points. Both are on the same clock, so we directly plot force versus displacement. We don't use the reflected wave for the displacement (too disturbed by the shape of the pusher).

**End of the measurement.** The transmitted wave keeps going to the free end of the output bar, reflects there as compression and comes back. When it passes under the gauge again, the gauge sees the outgoing plus the returning wave and we can no longer read the force. That's the only limit on the test duration: T = 2(L_output − gauge distance)/c. The striker is chosen so the pulse lasts about T, i.e. L_striker = L_output − gauge distance.

**End of the pulse.** When the unloading wave from the striker reaches the pusher, nothing compresses the input bar/pusher contact anymore while the reflected wave is tensile: the pusher separates from the input bar.

**The echo in the input bar.** The reflected wave travels back up the input bar to its left end. The striker has already separated, so it's a free end: the wave comes back as compression and reloads the specimen a second time. Not a problem as long as it arrives after the useful test, which is the case if L_input ≥ L_striker. No need for more, unlike a classic SHPB, because we don't measure anything in the input bar: it doesn't matter if the waves overlap there.

**Why it's compact.** Only the output bar limits the duration, and it's folded above the input bar. For the same test duration it's 50% shorter than a classic Kolsky bar, or for the same length the test lasts twice as long, which makes it possible to go all the way to fracture at around 100 s⁻¹.
