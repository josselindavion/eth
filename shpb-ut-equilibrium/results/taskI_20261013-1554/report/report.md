# Task I - Force equilibrium and loading speeds in the SHPB with load inversion device

Study `taskI_20261013-1554`, specimens: UT19. Generated by `scripts/post/report_taskI.py`; all numbers in the results sections are computed from the simulation outputs.

Questions of Task I: (1) can quasi-static force equilibrium be achieved in the specimen at nominal strain rates of 100, 500 and 1000 s⁻¹, and (2) which loading speeds are required to reach an engineering strain of 30 %?

## Signal processing and definitions

The simulated signals are processed as in the experiments. Forces and strain are low-pass filtered (4th-order Butterworth, 100 kHz cut-off, zero phase), the cut-off used for the output bar strain gauge signal in the laboratory (Roth et al. 2015): the simulation writes one point per microsecond and contains vibrations of a few microseconds that the experiment cannot resolve. The effect of the filter is shown in Table T2.

The force in the specimen is taken in two cross-sections at both ends of the parallel section (F_in on the load inversion device side, F_out on the output bar side). These exact forces are used to answer the equilibrium question. In the experiment only the strain gauge of the output bar is available (F = A E ε); its simulated signal is shifted in time by 400 mm / 4871 m/s = 82 µs to the specimen (no dispersion correction) and used only in the section "What the experiment will measure". The engineering strain is measured by a 12 mm virtual extensometer on the parallel section, equivalent to the DIC extensometer.

Force equilibrium is quantified by R = (F_in - F_out) / F_mean with F_mean = (F_in + F_out) / 2, and the specimen is considered in equilibrium when |R| < 5 %. The equilibrium strain is the strain from which |R| stays below this limit until 30 % strain (or until the end of loading). The loading start is the first instant where F_in exceeds 2 % of its maximum.

The achieved strain rate is the slope of the extensometer strain between 5 % strain and min(25 %, 0.8 x maximum strain), i.e. the strain gained in this interval divided by the time taken. The interval excludes the start-up transient (rise of the incident pulse, settling of the specimen shoulders in the pockets, build-up of the specimen force up to its maximum at about 5 % strain) and the end of the loading, so that it covers the established regime described by the striker velocity estimate (section "Loading speeds"). The complete strain rate history is given in F4 and other definitions in Table T3.

### Force equilibrium

![F1](F1_equilibrium.png)

**Figure F1 - Force equilibrium in the specimen.** *Plotted:* the equilibrium ratio R = (F_in - F_out) / F_mean (vertical axis) against the engineering strain of the specimen (horizontal axis), one curve per target rate. *Where it comes from:* F_in and F_out are the exact axial forces in two cross-sections of the simulated specimen, at both ends of its parallel section (F_in on the load inversion device side, F_out on the output bar side), filtered at 100 kHz; they are not measured by a strain gauge. The strain is given by the 12 mm virtual extensometer on the specimen. *How to read it:* when a curve is inside the shaded band (|R| < 5 %), both ends of the specimen carry the same force: the specimen is in equilibrium. The large values below about 2 % strain come from the start of loading (specimen shoulders settling in the pockets) and are cut at ±20 %.

- **UT19, 100 s⁻¹** (striker 2.48 m/s): force equilibrium (|R| < 5 %) is established at 0.9 % strain (0.29 ms after loading start) and holds until the end of the loading (max |R| after 5 % strain: 0.9 %).
- **UT19, 500 s⁻¹** (striker 7.69 m/s): force equilibrium (|R| < 5 %) is established at 2.5 % strain (0.12 ms after loading start) and holds until the end of the loading (max |R| after 5 % strain: 3.8 %).
- **UT19, 1000 s⁻¹** (striker 14.20 m/s): force equilibrium is **only partly reached**: |R| stays below 5 % from 2.7 % to 13.7 % strain, but reaches 13.2 % at other instants after 5 % strain (|F_in - F_out| up to 0.60 kN, i.e. 8.0 % of the maximum force).

In all cases the first few tens of microseconds show a short force pulse followed by a drop to zero before continuous loading starts (F2). This is most likely the impact of the specimen shoulders in the clamping pockets, the specimen being inserted without pre-load; it produces the large values of R below about 2 % strain in F1.

The maximum force is reached at about 5 to 8 % strain; the force then decreases (necking). The same force difference therefore gives a larger ratio R late in the test, which is why the maximum absolute difference |F_in - F_out| after 5 % strain is also given in Table T1.

**Figure F4 - Strain rate in the specimen.** *Plotted:* the strain rate (vertical axis, logarithmic scale) against the engineering strain (horizontal axis), one curve per target rate; dotted horizontal lines: target rates; dots: force maximum (onset of necking). *Where it comes from:* time derivative of the strain of the 12 mm virtual extensometer on the specimen (strain filtered at 100 kHz before derivation). After the force maximum the strain rate is established close to the target and increases slowly, because the specimen force decreases (necking) and brakes the bench less. ![F5](F5_striker_velocity.png)

**Figure F5 - Striker velocity required for a given strain rate.** *Plotted:* the striker velocity (vertical axis), i.e. the setting of the test, against the strain rate in the specimen (horizontal axis). Line: the estimate V_striker = (ε̇ × L_eff + 2F/ρcA) / (2 × 0.487) explained below, used to choose the velocities of the simulations. Dots: the simulations, placed at the striker velocity that was imposed and at the strain rate that was achieved (5-25 % window, Table T3). Shaded region: strain rates for which 30 % strain cannot be reached with the 5 m striker. *How to read it:* choose a strain rate on the horizontal axis and read the striker velocity on the line. Dots lying on the line confirm the estimate.

The striker velocity is estimated from the elongation rate of the load inversion device, v ≈ 2 v_i - 2 F / (ρ c A), where v_i = 0.487 V_striker is the particle velocity of the incident wave (impedance ratio of striker and bar) and F the specimen force: the specimen force both slows the inverter down and pushes the output bar. Hence V_striker = (ε̇ × L_eff + 2F/ρcA) / (2 × 0.487), with L_eff = 12.7 mm and F = 7 kN for the UT19. L_eff is an effective gauge length relating the elongation rate to the extensometer strain rate; it was calibrated on a first simulation at 500 s⁻¹, and F is the typical specimen force (6.4 to 7.6 kN in the simulations). A first estimate without the force term (V = ε̇ × 15 mm) gave only 21 s⁻¹ instead of 100 s⁻¹, because the specimen force is then almost as large as the force carried by the incident wave.

**Limit for 30 % strain.** The 5 m striker gives a 2.12 ms loading pulse, so 30 % strain requires at least 0.30 / 2.12 ms ≈ 142 s⁻¹ (shaded region in F5); the output bar (5.99 m) also limits the measurement window to 2.33 ms (Beerli et al. 2026). At 100 s⁻¹ (UT19), the strain stops at 18.5 %; reaching 30 % would need a loading of about 3.1 ms (0.30 / 105 s⁻¹ + 0.28 ms start-up), i.e. a striker of about 7.4 m, almost the length of the 7.5 m launch tube (no room left to accelerate it), and a loading longer than the measurement window of the output bar.

## What the experiment will measure

In the experiment the force is only known from the output bar gauge. Figures F2 and F3 compare this signal, as it would be recorded, with the exact force in the specimen.

Coloured solid line: exact force in the input section of the specimen (F_in); coloured dashed line: exact force in the output section (F_out); black line: force given by the virtual strain gauge on the output bar, as recorded in the experiment. *Where it comes from:* the gauge force is the axial stress of the gauge elements times the bar cross-section, shifted in time by 82 µs (travel time of the wave over the 400 mm between the specimen and the gauge) so that it can be compared with the specimen forces; all signals are filtered at 100 kHz. *How to read it:* when the two coloured lines overlap, the specimen is in equilibrium; the distance between the black line and the coloured lines is the measurement error of the gauge.

**Figure F3 - Engineering stress-strain curves.** *Plotted:* engineering stress = force / initial cross-section of the parallel section (A0 = 7.5 mm² for the UT19) on the vertical axis, against the engineering strain of the 12 mm extensometer on the horizontal axis; one colour per target rate. Solid line: force from the output bar gauge, i.e. the curve that the experiment will give. As the simulated material is rate independent, the dotted curves of the three rates coincide. The vertical axis is cut at 1600 MPa (spike of the gauge signal below 2 % strain).

- UT19, 100 s⁻¹: maximum force 7.50 kN at 5.8 % strain; the output bar gauge deviates from the specimen force by up to 3.1 % of the maximum force after 5 % strain.
- UT19, 500 s⁻¹: maximum force 7.56 kN at 5.0 % strain; the output bar gauge deviates from the specimen force by up to 15.0 % of the maximum force after 5 % strain.
- UT19, 1000 s⁻¹: maximum force 7.58 kN at 7.6 % strain; the output bar gauge deviates from the specimen force by up to 38.9 % of the maximum force after 5 % strain.

The force in the specimen is smooth, whereas the output bar gauge signal oscillates around it, with an amplitude that grows with the loading speed. An oscillating measured curve therefore does not mean that the specimen is out of equilibrium. The origin of these oscillations has not been identified yet: they appear between the specimen and the gauge and may come from the clamping head of the output bar or from its modelling (tetrahedral mesh tied to the bar). This must be checked before comparing with the experiments.

## Limitations

- Rate-independent specimen material: in the experiments the force level, and hence the required striker velocity (term 2F/ρcA), will increase with the strain rate.
- The striker velocity estimate relies on L_eff calibrated on one simulation and on a constant specimen force F.
- The output bar gauge signal is shifted, not corrected for dispersion; the exact reference of the 400 mm gauge distance is to be confirmed (about 6 µs uncertainty).
- The origin of the oscillations of the output bar gauge (section "What the experiment will measure") is not identified yet.
- Assumed values: densities of the bars and of the striker, material of the pusher (maraging).
- The long-gauge specimen (UT40) is not simulated yet (geometry not available).

- Equilibrium is reached early and maintained at the lower rates; at the highest rate it is only partly reached, the largest differences occurring late in the test, when the force decreases.
- The striker velocity must account for the specimen force (V_striker formula, F5); 30 % strain cannot be reached below about 142 s⁻¹ with the 5 m striker.
- Even when the specimen is in equilibrium, the output bar gauge oscillates around the specimen force: this has to be considered when assessing the quality of the measured curves.
